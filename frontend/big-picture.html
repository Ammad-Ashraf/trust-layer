If this particle existed, how many would CMS have counted?

Every Collider-Bench task comes down to that one number. An AI agent answers it by simulating collisions on a computer. Our checker makes sure the arithmetic behind the number is right.

In the fishing picture: how common the fish is, times how much water you swept, times the share that are the right species, times how many the net keeps.

Why "in 2016"

The LHC runs in multi-year runs. Our tasks use one year of Run 2.

The LHC collects data in long periods called runs, with the machine settings kept steady. In 2016 the beams collided at 13 TeV, and CMS recorded 35.9 fb⁻¹ of good data. All three of our tasks are CMS searches that used exactly that dataset. That is why every task says "35.9 fb⁻¹".

How much data. Think of it as how much rain fell per square metre: the more rain, the more drops land in any bucket.

How to read it 1 fb × 1 fb⁻¹ = 1

A process with a 1 fb cross-section happens about once per 1 fb⁻¹ of data. In 35.9 fb⁻¹ it happens about 36 times, before any filters.

The fishing contest

CMS fished the 2016 lake and caught nothing unusual. To say "species X does not live here", they first worked out how many X they would have caught if it did. If that number is big and they caught none, X is ruled out. Collider-Bench asks the AI to redo that "how many would we have caught" calculation with public tools. CMS's own number is the answer key, and the AI never sees it.

The three tasks

Three searches, three imagined particles

The particles are not real (as far as anyone knows). They come from supersymmetry, a popular idea that every known particle has a heavier partner. Each task fixes one "benchmark": exact particles and exact masses. Pick a task, and the collision drawing at the top changes to what that particle would leave in the detector.
