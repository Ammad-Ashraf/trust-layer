Lesson 1 · what gets smashed

A proton is a small bag of quarks

Everything around you is made of atoms. In the middle of every atom sits a tiny core, and in that core are protons. The LHC smashes protons into each other.

A proton is not solid. It is a bag of three quarks, held together by gluons, the "glue" particles. When two protons collide, really it's a quark or gluon from one hitting a quark or gluon from the other.

Lesson 2 · the unit GeV

Particles are weighed in GeV

Physicists weigh particles in GeV ("giga-electron-volts"). It is really an energy unit, but since Einstein's E = mc² says mass and energy are the same thing, it works as a weight too.

The easy anchor: a proton weighs about 1 GeV. If a proton were a 1 kg bag of sugar, everything else on this scale is weighed against it.

Why it matters

To make a heavy particle, a collision has to "spend" at least its weight in energy. One LHC collision has a budget of 13,000 GeV, but it's shared among the quarks inside, so making very heavy things is rare.

Lesson 3 · what the detector sees

The detector is an onion, and each particle stops in its own layer

CMS is a giant cylinder built in layers around the collision point. A particle flies outward and leaves a different mark in each layer. Physicists identify it from where it stops and what it leaves behind.

Lesson 4 · missing energy

How do you see something invisible? Check the balance.

The protons fly straight along the beam, so before the collision nothing is moving sideways. After the collision, all the sideways pushes must still cancel out. That's a law of nature (momentum is conserved).

So if the visible pieces all lean one way, something we can't see must have flown the other way. The size of that imbalance is called missing energy, written ETmiss. Invisible particles, like the neutrino and the imagined ones, show up only this way.

Lesson 5 · the imagined particles

Supersymmetry: every particle might have a heavy shadow twin

Supersymmetry (SUSY) is a popular idea, not a proven fact: every known particle may have a heavier twin. Nobody has found one yet. The three CMS searches in our tasks were looking for them, and found none.

The names follow two rules. Twins of matter particles get an s- in front: quark → squark, top quark → top squark. Twins of force particles get -ino at the end: gluon → gluino. The twins of the W, Z, photon and Higgs mix together into charginos (with electric charge) and neutralinos (without).

The key rule for us: a heavy twin decays down a staircase into lighter twins, giving off normal particles at each step. The lightest twin can't decay any further, so it flies out of the detector unseen. That's where the missing energy comes from. Some physicists think it could even be dark matter.

Lesson 7 · how rare, and the units

A few special grains in a thousand swimming pools of sand

In 2016, CMS recorded roughly 2.8 million billion proton collisions. If our 1750 GeV gluinos existed, about 142 pairs would have been made among all of them, and about 55 would have passed the filters.

That's why the counting formula matters. You can't just look; you have to predict the number exactly and compare.

Picture it

Make every collision one grain of sand (about 1 mm³). The 2016 collisions would fill about 1,100 Olympic swimming pools. Somewhere in there are 142 special grains.

The four units you'll meet
GeVWeight (or energy) of a particle. Proton ≈ 1 GeV.
TeV1,000 GeV. The LHC collides at 13 TeV.
fb"How likely": the size of the target a process offers. Smaller = rarer. 1 pb = 1,000 fb.
fb⁻¹"How much data": how much collision "rain" fell. fb × fb⁻¹ = a count.
